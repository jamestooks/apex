# Apex follows UK advice, but needs tuning

Measured against UK ADHD guidance, Apex matches the low-intensity, family-level support that NICE NG87, the NHS and UK charities recommend for school-age children unusually closely. It teaches about ADHD-type patterns in plain language, gives parents shared calm phrases, uses short focus blocks with movement breaks, plans coping strategies when the child is calm, models repair after blow-ups, and produces a plan card the school's SENCo can use. Its 15-minute cap, enforced rest, on-device data and lack of streaks, ads or notifications go further than any UK screen-time or ICO Children's Code requirement for a home-made game. Where it diverges, the problems come from a few specific mechanics, not from the concept. Fixed 13–21-second waits run straight into the best-known UK research on delay aversion. A "pack trust" score that falls uses loss aversion on a child who may be highly sensitive to rejection. Alarm-style heartbeat, growl and red edges can raise arousal instead of cueing calm. Trophies that arrive only after a parent confirms them come too late for a delay-averse child. A hard stop at 15 minutes risks setting off the meltdown the game is trying to prevent. Unqualified "gift" lines go against ADHD UK's language advice. The evidence ceiling matters most of all. UK-led meta-analyses show that games and cognitive training improve what they train, but have little or no effect on everyday ADHD symptoms when raters don't know who got the intervention. The two games closest to Apex only showed benefits as add-ons to therapy or usual care, with an adult involved. Apex's "not a treatment" label is therefore correct and should stay. Its best case is as a vehicle for parent–child practice and shared language, which is where UK evidence is strongest. The family should judge it by what happens at home and school, not by how he plays in the game.

## Apex already fits the first rung of the NICE and NHS ladder

NICE NG87 (2018, updated 2019) sets out a stepped sequence for children aged 5 and over. First comes ADHD-focused support for the family, which "can be group based and as few as 1 or 2 sessions" and covers education about ADHD, parenting strategies and, with consent, liaison with school. Next come environmental modifications, then parent training, then medication if symptoms persist, then CBT for young people who are still significantly impaired ([NICE NG87 recommendations, via search extract](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations); [NICE draft short version](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/documents/short-version-of-draft-guideline)). NICE's examples of environmental modification include **"shorter periods of focus with movement breaks"** and reducing distractions ([NICE NG87, via search extract](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations)). Apex's design maps onto almost every element of this first rung. The chapters explain the patterns, the shared phrases are parenting strategies, the plan cards support school liaison, and Burst & Rest plus the session cap are movement breaks and short focus periods. The skills it rehearses (self-control, handling and expressing feelings, problem-solving) are the same ones NICE lists as CBT content ([NICE NG87 PDF, via search extract](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/resources/attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-diagnosis-and-management-pdf-1837699732933)). Apex should not call itself CBT, though. NICE frames CBT as a clinician-delivered therapy for older young people, offered later in the sequence.

The NHS's everyday parenting advice reads almost like an Apex design document. It tells parents to split tasks into **"15 to 20 minute slots with a break in between each slot"**, give instructions "one at a time in a calm voice", use praise or a reward chart, put a to-do list "somewhere easy to see", make time for physical activity, and talk to the SENCo about adjustments ([NHS: Living with ADHD](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-adhd/living-with/); [NHS: ADHD treatment](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-adhd/treatment/)). The 15-minute cap, the calm shared phrases, the fridge plan card, the parent-confirmed missions and the movement breaks each correspond to one of these lines.

UK charity and clinician advice on meltdowns points the same way. YoungMinds tells parents not to meet anger with anger. It suggests separating the feeling from the behaviour ("it's okay that you're angry, but it's not okay to shout at me"), working out triggers and choosing coping tools together "when things are calm", checking in gently afterwards, modelling apology, and then **"move forward"** without dwelling ([YoungMinds anger guide](https://www.youngminds.org.uk/parent/parents-a-z-mental-health-guide/anger/)). Apex's "rage tells you something matters" line, its reflection question ("what happened just before?"), its repair scene and its short one-next-step failure cards all track that sequence. Dr Max Davie's five pillars are positive responses to positive behaviour, a few consistent rules, sleep, family meals and daily exercise, and his insistence that ADHD is "not their fault" sits well with Apex's neutral field notes ([ARCS Lambeth, 2017](https://www.arcslambeth.co.uk/my-child-has-adhd-is-it-my-fault/)). The Anna Freud Centre's advice to focus on "positive conversations and rewards, rather than looking at the negatives", to ask about the child's experience rather than give opinions, and to keep in regular contact with school is the same philosophy ([Anna Freud](https://www.annafreud.org/resources/family-wellbeing/supporting-treatments/supporting-children-and-young-people-with-adhd/)).

In school, the SEND Code of Practice's graduated approach (assess, plan, do, review) expects staff to talk to parents first and involve parents and children in planning, with the SENCo coordinating ([Cheshire East Local Offer](https://www.cheshireeast.gov.uk/livewell/local-offer-for-children-with-sen-and-disabilities/education/supporting-send-in-education/sen-and-disability-in-the-early-years/sen-support-the-graduated-approach.aspx); [NASEN summary](https://www.scanningpens.co.uk/site/downloadable-resources/primary/NASEN-SEND-Code-of-practice%20summary.pdf)). A parent-and-child-authored plan card is exactly the kind of input the "assess" and "plan" stages can use. Support does not depend on a diagnosis, and the NHS England ADHD Taskforce explicitly wants school support "based on their needs without waiting for a diagnosis" ([NHS England Taskforce Part 2](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/report-of-the-independent-adhd-taskforce-part-2/)).

On screens and design ethics, Apex goes beyond what UK bodies require. No UK body sets a time limit for school-age children. The UK Chief Medical Officers (2019) found the evidence insufficient for an "optimal amount", and the RCPCH asks families four questions about control, displacement, sleep and snacking, with no screens in the hour before bed ([GOV.UK CMO commentary](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-cmo-commentary-on-screen-time-and-social-media-map-of-reviews); [Childnet summary of RCPCH](https://www.childnet.com/help-and-advice/screen-time-boundaries-parents/what-the-experts-say/)). UK researchers at Oxford find that time spent gaming has effects on wellbeing "probably too small to be subjectively noticeable" ([OII](https://www.oii.ox.ac.uk/major-new-study-finds-little-evidence-for-causal-connection-between-well-being-and-video-game-playing/)). Fifteen minutes of Apex is not a screen-time concern in itself. The ICO Children's Code almost certainly does not apply to a free, offline game for one family. Its "information society service" scope and its reliance on personal-data processing both point that way ([ICO: services covered](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/services-covered-by-this-code/)). Used as a voluntary benchmark, it shows Apex meeting the hard standards: no **"reward loops, continuous scrolling, notifications and auto-play"**, no profiling, data kept on the device, and enforced breaks that match the ICO's advice to nudge 6–9-year-olds toward "taking breaks" ([ICO Standard 5](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/5-detrimental-use-of-data/); [ICO Standard 13](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/13-nudge-techniques/)). Trophies that are fixed, earned by effort and never random are the opposite of a loot box.

## Blinded trials cap what any game can claim

The most important UK contribution here is methodological. The European ADHD Guidelines Group, led largely from King's College London and Southampton, has shown repeatedly that non-drug ADHD interventions look effective when rated by parents who know their child received them, and that **the effects shrink or vanish when raters are probably blinded**. The 2023 update on computerised cognitive training, covering 36 RCTs, found a probably-blinded effect on total ADHD symptoms of **SMD 0.12 (95% CI −0.01 to 0.25), effectively nil**. Working memory improved, but academic and other outcomes did not ([Westwood et al., Mol Psychiatry 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36977764/)). Neurofeedback fared no better: "evidence from well-controlled trials with probably blinded outcomes currently fails to support" it ([Cortese et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27238063/)). NICE reviewed both and recommended neither ([PMC review, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10091126)). The one NICE-assessed digital ADHD technology, QbTest, supports *assessment*, and NICE says it should "supplement professional judgement, and not replace it" ([NICE news](https://www.nice.org.uk/news/articles/nice-recommends-digital-technology-to-help-diagnose-adhd-in-children-and-young-people)). In November 2025 the Taskforce asked NICE to scope an early value assessment of digital ADHD management products ([NHS England Taskforce Part 2](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/report-of-the-independent-adhd-taskforce-part-2/)). That request confirms that no UK benchmark yet exists for anything like Apex.

The games closest to Apex fit this pattern. RAGE-Control, tested in a small double-blind RCT of 40 young people aged 10–17, was played at the end of therapist-led Anger Control Training. Compared with a sham game, it improved aggression (ES 0.55) and oppositionality (ES 0.48). However, **self-reported anger fell equally in both arms**: the game changed what children did with anger, not how angry they felt ([Ducharme et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34539455/)). Its active ingredient was real heart-rate feedback. Without sensors, Apex is structurally closer to the sham arm, which still improved alongside therapy. So whatever Apex achieves will most likely come from rehearsal, metaphor and adult involvement, not biofeedback. Plan-It Commander improved parent-rated time management in 170 children aged 8–12, but only as an add-on to usual treatment, and in an open-label trial ([Bul et al., JMIR 2016](https://www.jmir.org/2016/2/e26/metrics)). EndeavorRx, the only FDA-authorised ADHD game, beat its control on a computerised attention test, but showed **no significant between-group difference on any of seven parent or clinician scales** ([FDA De Novo review](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN200026.pdf); [sponsor evidence page](https://www.endeavorotc.com/evidence/stars-adhd-pivotal-study/)). A 2025 review of 22 ADHD games judged far transfer "unconvincing" for every game element, narrative included ([Schultz et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41122750/)).

The individual coping tools have plausible rationales but thin ADHD-specific evidence. The table below summarises how strong the support is for each part of Apex.

| Apex element | Closest UK-relevant evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Parent phrases, co-play, "do it with them" | Behavioural parent training: blinded gains in parenting and conduct problems ([Daley et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25062591/)); improves children's emotional symptoms ([Guo et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/10870547211001953)) | Moderate–strong (for parenting, not for game play) |
| Burst & Rest movement breaks | Meta-analyses show executive-function gains; effect sizes (e.g. SMD 2.19) implausibly large ([Sun et al. 2024](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cch.13150)) | Moderate, low quality |
| Long Exhale (3 in, 5 out) | Slow paced breathing drives HRV biofeedback ([Blum et al.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6763967/)); no ADHD-specific RCT found | Weak, plausible, low-risk |
| One Thing grounding | 5-4-3-2-1 grounding in ACAMH practitioner guidance ([ACAMH 2026](https://www.acamh.org/blog/supporting-emotion-regulation-in-adolescents-with-adhd-adhd-symptoms/)) | Expert opinion |
| Family mindfulness | Rubia's family meditation study: about 35% parent-rated gain, uncontrolled and unblinded ([KCL](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/sahaja-yoga-meditation-as-a-family-treatment-programme-for-children-with-attention-deficithyperactivity-disorder(b4f76c45-125c-49bf-8a60-635295b43a0c).html)) | Weak |
| In-game skill transfer to real life | Cognitive training and games: no blinded symptom effect ([Westwood et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36977764/)) | Strong evidence *against* assuming transfer |

Emotional dysregulation is a well-chosen target. It affects roughly **25–45% of children with ADHD** and predicts worse outcomes, but the overlap is "far from complete" ([Shaw et al. 2014](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2013.13070966)). Not every instinct in the game will fit this particular boy. The personal warning signs partly handle this, and a clinician could help confirm which chapters matter most for him.

## Six mechanics pull against the evidence

The **13–21-second waits** in the impulse chapter are the clearest mismatch. Edmund Sonuga-Barke's delay-aversion model, the most influential UK theory of ADHD motivation, holds that "the imposition of fixed delay creates frustration and emotional arousal" and that ADHD behaviours intensify as delay grows. It recommends building tolerance through **"shaping and fading procedures"** and "gradual exposure" ([Delay Aversion and ADHD](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/12827912.pdf)). Delay aversion activates the amygdala during cued waits, so for these children waiting is an emotional event, not just a cognitive one ([Van Dessel et al. 2018](https://acamh.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcpp.12868)). It is also made worse by "negative social interactions during waiting-related encounters" ([JCPP 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39710599/)). Fixed waits of 13–21 seconds from the first attempt are long for a delay-averse 9-year-old. Giving him something to do during the wait (breathing, watching the urge-wave) is consistent with the model. The starting length is the problem.

The **pack trust score that can go down** is the second. Apex's failure cards ("what happened plus one next step") fit UK advice well, but a falling relationship score is different. Loss-framed mechanics use loss aversion. For an emotionally dysregulated child, losing standing with the pack or with Scar can feel like relational rejection. UK parent advice on rejection sensitivity urges being "the safe place, not the detective" and stresses that "small, specific evidence of capability is what sticks" ([ADHD Pathfinder](https://www.adhdpathfinder.co.uk/post/rejection-sensitive-dysphoria-in-children-and-teens-with-adhd)). Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria is not a formal diagnosis and has a thin research base, and no study has tested loss framing in ADHD games. So this is a design judgement, not a proven harm. It is still the mechanic most likely to produce shame or a takeover.

The **heartbeat, growl, red edges and screen shake** are the third. Accessibility guidance recommends that intense effects be optional and *independently* adjustable, with flashing kept under 3 per second across 25% of the screen ([Game Accessibility Guidelines summary](https://abratabia.com/game-accessibility/)). Apex's reduced-motion and low-intensity settings meet the letter of this. The deeper issue is that the effects are designed to *mirror and amplify* rising arousal. RAGE-Control created pressure through task difficulty, not alarming sensory effects ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/930620)). Even EndeavorRx, a gentle game, produced frustration in 3% of children and emotional reactions in 1% ([sponsor evidence page](https://www.endeavorotc.com/evidence/stars-adhd-pivotal-study/)). Nobody has tested alarm-style cues on children with ADHD. Whether they help this boy notice his state or push him over the lock line is an empirical question his parents can answer by watching.

**Delayed, parent-confirmed trophies** are the fourth. Rewarding effort is strongly supported, but meta-analysis confirms that children with ADHD discount delayed rewards more steeply than other children ([Marx, Cortese, Sonuga-Barke et al. 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1087054718772138)). A trophy confirmed days later motivates weakly. Parent confirmation also risks making the parent an auditor rather than a co-regulator. Dr Mine Conkbayir argues that self-regulation is "diametrically opposed to behaviour management strategies" built on reward and sanction ([The Voice of Early Childhood](https://thevoiceofearlychildhood.com/self-regulation-co-regulation-and-mental-health/)).

The **hard 15-minute stop** is the fifth. ADHD is moderately associated with gaming-disorder symptoms (r ≈ 0.30 across 41 samples, mostly cross-sectional) ([Koncz et al. 2023](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/the-emerging-evidence-on-the-association-between-symptoms-of-adhd/)). The specific ADHD risk is difficulty stopping, not total minutes. A cut-off in the middle of a Hunt or with the meter high could trigger exactly the transition meltdown the game is meant to help with. Staged warnings, visible timers and a predictable post-play routine are standard practitioner advice, though they rest on opinion rather than trials ([ADDitude](https://www.additudemag.com/managing-transitions-adhd-children/)). The ICO also recommends "pause buttons which allow children to take a break at any time without losing their progress" ([ICO Standard 5](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/5-detrimental-use-of-data/)).

**Strengths framing** is the sixth, and the most contested. UK voices genuinely disagree. The ADHD Foundation's Dr Tony Lloyd embraces "superpower" language ([Good News Liverpool](https://www.goodnewsliverpool.co.uk/2021/03/18/adhd-foundation-launches-inspirational-comic/)). ADHD UK's style guide warns against it *"without qualifying how challenging ADHD can be"* ([ADHD UK style guide](https://adhduk.co.uk/about-us/press/adhd-uk-adhd-language-style-guide-reccomendations-on-writing-about-adhd/)). Prof Susan Young says no patient she treats would call it a superpower "because that completely undermines the struggle" ([The Psychologist](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/adhd-presented-quirky-thing-its-almost-become-entertainment)). Sonuga-Barke and Thapar take neurodiversity seriously while asking whether it helps clinicians at all ([Lancet Psychiatry 2021](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/143266/)). "Rage tells you something matters" holds up well, because it validates the feeling without denying the cost. "Your senses never switch off — a gift" is weaker when it stands alone. The "master/tame your instincts" framing could also read as compliance, as though the child's nature is wild and must be subdued. Kirby's "spiky profiles" and Sonuga-Barke's view that impairment is partly context-dependent ([JCPP 2023](https://acamh.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcpp.13710)) suggest showing the environment adapting too. Apex does acknowledge real impairment, since instincts can "take over", and that balance is a strength.

Two smaller risks complete the picture. People with ADHD tend to be *less* accurate at reading body signals such as heartbeat ([interoception systematic review, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11842156/)). The personal warning signs therefore need an outside observer's help, not just self-report. And because the clearest UK screen rule is no screens in the hour before bed ([Childnet summary of RCPCH](https://www.childnet.com/help-and-advice/screen-time-boundaries-parents/what-the-experts-say/)), evening practice of Long Exhale should happen off-screen.

## Concrete changes to the game and to family use

None of the changes below means rebuilding the game. Most are tuning, and several cost nothing because they are about how the family uses it.

| Change | Why (UK source) |
|---|---|
| Start Wait for the Signal at about 3–5 seconds and lengthen it gradually based on success; drop back after a miss | Delay-aversion "shaping and fading" ([Sonuga-Barke review](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/12827912.pdf)) |
| Make pack trust rise-only or "paused", show worry through story ("the herd is spooked") rather than a number, and always show the repair route straight away; Scar's trust never falls | RSD-informed advice ([ADHD Pathfinder](https://www.adhdpathfinder.co.uk/post/rejection-sensitive-dysphoria-in-children-and-teens-with-adhd)); YoungMinds "move forward" ([YoungMinds](https://www.youngminds.org.uk/parent/parents-a-z-mental-health-guide/anger/)) |
| Split intensity into separate sliders (heartbeat/growl audio, red vignette, shake), default to moderate, never flash, and make effects audibly *fall* when a tool is used so the reward is calm | Accessibility guidance ([GAG summary](https://abratabia.com/game-accessibility/)); EndeavorRx adverse events ([sponsor](https://www.endeavorotc.com/evidence/stars-adhd-pivotal-study/)) |
| Give instant in-game acknowledgement when a mission attempt is logged (a Scar line naming the tool), with the trophy added later on top | Steep discounting of delayed rewards ([Marx et al. 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1087054718772138)) |
| Add 3- and 1-minute warnings, end at a natural break, close with one breathing round and a suggested off-screen next step; add a pause that keeps progress | Transition practice ([ADDitude](https://www.additudemag.com/managing-transitions-adhd-children/)); ICO Standard 5 ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/5-detrimental-use-of-data/)) |
| Pair every strength line with its cost ("a gift on the hunt, exhausting in a crowded den"); swap "tame" for "steer" or "work with"; never use "superpower" | ADHD UK style guide ([ADHD UK](https://adhduk.co.uk/about-us/press/adhd-uk-adhd-language-style-guide-reccomendations-on-writing-about-adhd/)); Prof Susan Young ([BPS](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/adhd-presented-quirky-thing-its-almost-become-entertainment)) |
| Let the child choose which warning signs appear on a printed card; label cards "written by [child] and parent — not clinical advice"; invite the SENCo to review | NICE consent-based school liaison ([NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations)); SEND co-production ([SENDIASS BCP](https://sendiass4bcp.org/support/parent-carer-resources/parent-carer-resources-special-educational-needs-and-disabilities-send-support-in-schools/)) |
| Add a child-friendly line telling him a grown-up can see the Den log and plan cards; give a younger sibling her own profile so siblings can't see each other's warning signs | ICO Standards 3 and 11 ([ICO code](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/)) |
| Prefix parent phrases with a parent self-regulation cue ("take your own exhale, then say it") | "We cannot co-regulate if we cannot self-regulate" ([Conkbayir](https://thevoiceofearlychildhood.com/self-regulation-co-regulation-and-mental-health/)) |

How the family uses Apex matters as much as the code. The evidence is best for parent involvement, so a parent should co-play at least the early sessions of each chapter. That is the only way to check he is actually breathing along, since the game has no sensor, and to see whether the alarm effects calm him or wind him up. Sessions should not be the first thing after school. UK neurodiversity charity SPACE Herts describes "after-school restraint collapse" and advises snack, movement and letting the child lead before any demands ([SPACE Herts](https://spaceherts.org.uk/2022/09/08/after-school-restraint-collapse/)). The reflection question works best at a calm moment, not straight after a hard day. Mission confirmation should feel like a celebration conversation that names the specific tool he used, not an audit. Shared phrases need a family rule that they are never used *at* him in an argument, especially by a sibling ("your rage meter's rising!"). Anna Freud advises explaining ADHD to siblings and giving them their own coping strategies ([Anna Freud](https://www.annafreud.org/resources/family-wellbeing/supporting-treatments/supporting-children-and-young-people-with-adhd/)). Finally, because parent ratings are unblinded, the family should judge progress by something outside the game. A simple weekly count of blow-ups and how quickly they ended, or a teacher's view from someone who doesn't know about the game, is more honest than in-game stats. Any "fuel" missions should stick to balanced, regular meals. NICE advises against general elimination diets and against fatty-acid supplements ([NICE NG87 dietary evidence](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/evidence/dietary-interventions-pdf-4844210798)).

## Questions worth taking to the GP, CAMHS and school

Apex had no clinician input. The cheapest way to close that gap is to bring the plan card and a few targeted questions to professionals the family already sees.

| Who | Question |
|---|---|
| GP / paediatrician | Does his pattern include significant emotional dysregulation, and which of the five instincts matter most for him? |
| GP / paediatrician | Is the family eligible for an ADHD-focused parent support or parent-training programme locally, as NICE NG87 describes, and is there a waiting list? |
| GP / paediatrician | Are there sensory sensitivities or co-occurring conditions (e.g. autism, anxiety) that would make heartbeat, growl and screen-shake effects a bad idea? |
| CAMHS / clinical psychologist | Could you look over the Long Exhale, One Thing, Park It and Wait for the Signal scripts and the warning-sign meter, and tell us whether anything conflicts with what you would teach? |
| CAMHS / clinical psychologist | What wait length is realistic for him to start with, and how should we build it up? |
| CAMHS / clinical psychologist | Should the repair scene and takeover consequences be changed if he shows strong reactions to perceived criticism? |
| CAMHS / clinical psychologist | If he later gets CBT or anger-management work, could Apex's language be aligned with it so he hears one vocabulary? |
| SENCo / class teacher | Can this plan card feed into his SEN Support assess–plan–do–review cycle, and when is the next review? |
| SENCo / class teacher | Will staff use the same signal and grown-up phrase, and is there a place and permission for a movement break or Long Exhale in class? |
| SENCo / class teacher | Would a teacher be willing to note changes over a half-term without knowing exactly what we're trying at home? |
| SENCo / class teacher | Is the school part of PINS or a Mental Health Support Team that could offer support without waiting for a diagnosis? |

## What this review could not see directly

Several limits affect how far these conclusions can be trusted. **nice.org.uk returned HTTP 403 to every request**, so all NG87 and QS39 content above comes from search-result extracts and secondary sources. Exact recommendation numbers were deliberately left out and should be checked first-hand before anyone quotes them to a clinician. The SEND Code of Practice was not read directly on gov.uk. Its description here comes from local-authority and NASEN summaries, and ongoing SEND reform may change the framework. The research also did not cover Scotland's, Wales's or Northern Ireland's separate additional-needs systems. The DfE's promised screen-use guidance for 5–16-year-olds, due in autumn 2026, could not be confirmed as published by 12 November 2026 ([GOV.UK, June 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/newguidance-on-screen-use-for-children-aged-5-16)). The RCPsych and YoungMinds ADHD parent pages returned 404s, and many PubMed Central full texts were blocked, so several trial figures come from abstracts. YoungMinds' advice on agreeing a "signal" appeared only in search snippets. The Game Accessibility Guidelines were seen through a secondary summary. No UK charity was found that addresses therapeutic games for children with ADHD. No study was found on loss-framed mechanics, alarm-style sensory cues or sensorless breathing games in children with ADHD, and none on how children themselves receive "superpower" framing. On those points, the recommendations above are reasoned inferences from neighbouring evidence, not tested findings. Much of the charity and clinician material is practice guidance or opinion rather than trial evidence, and one widely cited UK parent site (ADHD and You) is pharma-sponsored.

## Conclusion

What changes in this review is where Apex's value is likely to sit. The UK evidence gives little reason to expect that playing will make his ADHD better. It gives good reason to expect that a parent who practises calm scripts with him, repairs after conflict, rewards specific effort and gets the school using the same words is doing what NICE's first rung and the parent-training literature actually support. Apex is most defensible as the *container* for that adult behaviour. That reverses the usual question: the most important design work is in the Den, the shared phrases and the plan cards, not in the chapters. The game's risks are concentrated in places where it borrows from commercial game design (loss meters, dramatic arousal effects, fixed difficulty, hard stops). The fixes all move it toward the same principle: calm should be the reward, and setbacks should cost nothing relational.

The honest path is to keep "not a treatment", tune the six mechanics, judge it by off-screen outcomes a teacher can see, and use it as a reason to get professional eyes on his plan rather than a substitute for them. If the family ever shares Apex beyond their own home, two things change. It could come into scope of the ICO Children's Code, and its claims would need the kind of blinded evaluation that even well-funded ADHD games have struggled to pass.
