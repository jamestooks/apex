# Games and Digital Tools for Children with ADHD: Evidence, Risks and Design Ethics (UK lens), applied to "Apex"

Research date: 12 Nov 2026. Evidence-strength labels: [RCT] randomised controlled trial; [MA] meta-analysis/systematic review; [Obs] observational/cross-sectional; [Reg] regulator/government document; [Op] expert/practitioner opinion or grey literature.

Method note: PubMed Central (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) full texts were blocked by reCAPTCHA during this session, so trial details come from abstracts (via Europe PMC API), publisher/sponsor pages and university pages. Where only a secondary source was available, that is flagged.

## 1. Do serious games / digital interventions help children with ADHD? (RAGE-Control, EndeavorRx, Plan-It Commander, cognitive-training meta-analyses, transfer, adult role)

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced games help only modestly, and mostly on things close to what the game trains. Gains on blinded measures of everyday ADHD symptoms are small or zero. The most positive findings come from games used **as an add-on to therapy or usual care, with an adult involved**: RAGE-Control was paired with therapist-led Anger Control Training, and Plan-It Commander was an adjunct to treatment as usual and linked play to real-life goals. Apex follows this "game plus real-world practice plus adult" model. On the evidence, it should be presented as a practice aid, not as a treatment.

### Cited Findings
**RAGE-Control (Boston Children's Hospital)**
- [RCT, small, "proof of concept"] Ducharme P, Kahn J, Vaudreuil C, et al., Gonzalez-Heydrich J (2021), *Frontiers in Psychiatry* 12:591906. 40 youths aged 10–17 with clinically significant anger dyscontrol were randomised to 10 sessions of Anger Control Training (ACT) plus RAGE-Control, or ACT plus a sham game. The study was double-blind. — [PubMed 34539455](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34539455/); [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2021.591906/full)
- In the sham arm the child wore a heart-rate monitor, but heart rate did not affect the game. In the active arm, heart rate drove the game. Both arms played at the end of each psychotherapy session. — [EurekAlert / Boston Children's press release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/930620)
- Results: ACT + RAGE-Control produced larger improvements in aggression (ES 0.55, p=0.015), oppositionality (ES 0.48, p=0.032) and global severity (ES 0.51, p=0.023). Self-reported anger fell similarly in both arms. The game reduced the **behavioural expression** of anger but not the **experience** of angry feelings, compared with sham. Median heart rate during play fell in the RAGE-Control group. — [Europe PMC abstract / PubMed 34539455](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34539455/); [Neuroscience News summary](https://neurosciencenews.com/gaming-biofeedback-stress-19419/)
- Earlier work: Kahn J, Ducharme P, Rotenberg A, Gonzalez-Heydrich J (2013), "'RAGE-Control': A Game to Build Emotional Strength", *Games for Health Journal*. This described the original pilot with youth in a psychiatric inpatient unit. — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/%22RAGE-Control%22:-A-Game-to-Build-Emotional-Strength.-Kahn-Ducharme/076eb37a371582adee0484bb5204c1feaab06336); [PDF](http://www.tmslab.org/publications/787.pdf)
- Trial registration: [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03270813](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03270813)

**EndeavorRx / AKL-T01 (Akili)**
- [RCT, pivotal] STARS-ADHD: 348 children aged 8–12 with ADHD at 20 US sites. Active AKL-T01 (n=180) was compared with a digital control (n=168). Dose was about 25 min/day, 5 days/week, for 4 weeks. — [EndeavorOTC evidence page (sponsor)](https://www.endeavorotc.com/evidence/stars-adhd-pivotal-study/)
- Primary endpoint: mean change in TOVA Attention Performance Index was 0.93 vs 0.03 (p=0.006). TOVA is a computerised attention test, so this is "near" to the training task. — [sponsor page](https://www.endeavorotc.com/evidence/stars-adhd-pivotal-study/); [FDA De Novo DEN200026 review](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN200026.pdf)
- Secondary outcomes (parent and clinician ratings: ADHD-RS, Impairment Rating Scale and others) improved within groups, but there was **no statistically significant between-group difference** on the 7 secondary parent/clinician scales. About 24% of children had a ≥30% reduction in ADHD-RS. 56% of parents said attention improved, and 48% showed improved impairment on the IRS. — [Akili/PureTech](https://news.puretechhealth.com/node/6341/pdf); [sponsor page](https://www.endeavorotc.com/evidence/stars-adhd-pivotal-study/)
- Published in *The Lancet Digital Health* (2020; Kollins et al.). The FDA granted De Novo authorisation in June 2020 as a prescription adjunct for ages 8–12. — [FDA DEN200026](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN200026.pdf); [DTx Alliance](https://dtxalliance.org/products/endeavor/)
- Adverse events: none serious and no discontinuations. Mild events were frustration (3%), headache (2%) and emotional reaction (1%). Adherence was about 83 of 100 sessions. — [sponsor page](https://www.endeavorotc.com/evidence/stars-adhd-pivotal-study/)
- Adolescent pivotal trial (2023): the company reported improved attention. This is a sponsor press release, not peer-reviewed in the source found. — [Akili news, Jan 2023](https://www.akiliinteractive.com/news-collection/2023/1/6/pivotal-trial-of-endeavorrx-in-adolescents-with-adhd-shows-robust-improvements-in-attention-and-broader-clinical-outcomes)

**Plan-It Commander (Netherlands/Belgium)**
- [RCT, open-label crossover] Bul KC, Kato PM, Van der Oord S, Danckaerts M, et al., Maras A (2016), *JMIR* 18(2):e26. 170 children aged 8–12 with ADHD over 20 weeks, as an adjunct to treatment as usual. The primary outcome, parent-rated time-management skills, improved significantly more in the game group (p=.004). Secondary gains were seen in responsibility and working memory. ISRCTN62056259. — [PubMed/Europe PMC abstract via JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2016/2/e26/metrics); [Erasmus repository](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/effects-of-plan-it-commander-on-behavioral-outcomes-for-children-/)
- Its design aimed at daily-life skills (time management, planning/organising, prosocial skills) and was developed with clinicians, parents and children. — [Bul et al., development paper](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Development-and-User-Satisfaction-of-%E2%80%9CPlan-It-a-for-Bul-Franken/01c2bb34429003528081beab826f26fc5fd1bf23); [Bul PhD thesis](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/95502/Proefschrift-kim-bul-FINAL.pdf)
- Caveat: the trial was open-label, and outcomes were parent-rated by parents who knew the allocation.

**Cognitive-training meta-analyses (European ADHD Guidelines Group, EAGG; UK-led authors)**
- [MA] Cortese S, Ferrin M, Brandeis D, et al. (2015), *JAACAP*. This pooled RCTs of cognitive training. Effects on ADHD symptoms were significant with unblinded raters but dropped sharply with probably-blinded raters (ADHD total SMD 0.20, 95% CI 0.01–0.40; inattention SMD 0.32, CI −0.01 to 0.66). Working-memory training specifically had no effect on ADHD symptoms, despite improving WM performance. — [KCL Pure](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/cognitive-training-for-attention-deficithyperactivity-disorder-me/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/272819688_Cognitive_Training_for_Attention-DeficitHyperactivity_Disorder_Meta-Analysis_of_Clinical_and_Neuropsychological_Outcomes_From_Randomized_Controlled_Trials)
- [MA] Westwood SJ, Parlatini V, Rubia K, Cortese S, Sonuga-Barke EJS, EAGG (2023), *Molecular Psychiatry* 28:1402–1414. 36 RCTs. Probably-blinded ADHD total: SMD 0.12 (CI −0.01 to 0.25), meaning **no effect**. There was a small effect on inattention (SMD 0.17). Working memory improved (verbal SMD 0.38; visuospatial 0.49), but other neuropsychological and academic outcomes did not. Westwood, Rubia and Sonuga-Barke are at King's College London; Cortese is at Southampton. — [PubMed 36977764](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36977764/); [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-023-02000-7)

**Recent reviews of ADHD serious games (2024–2026)**
- [Systematic review/content analysis] Schultz et al. (2025): 37 studies (2005–2021) covering 22 ADHD games. Changes in parent-rated symptoms varied widely, with no obvious advantage for any game element. The authors judged "far transfer effects … unconvincing for seminal game elements". — [PubMed 41122750](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41122750/) (summary from search snippet; full text not retrieved)
- [Systematic review] Doulou et al. (2025), *Multimodal Technologies and Interaction* 9(1):8. 30 articles (2013–2024). The authors conclude that tech-based serious games can support executive function, metacognitive and emotional skills. This is a more optimistic narrative review with less weight on blinding. — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2414-4088/9/1/8)
- [Systematic review] *JMIR Serious Games* 2025 (e60937): serious games as digital therapeutics for children with ADHD. — [JMIR Serious Games](https://games.jmir.org/2025/1/e60937)
- [MA] 2025 meta-analyses on game-based interventions (Psychiatry Research, on exercise and neurofeedback elements; Eur Child Adolesc Psychiatry, on experiential-learning features). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165178125000964); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00787-025-02788-5) (abstracts not retrieved; listed for completeness)

### Inferences
- There is a consistent pattern: games improve what they train (TOVA score, working-memory tasks, the targeted skill rated by parents who know the allocation). Blinded effects on everyday ADHD symptoms are small or nil. Apex should therefore measure success by real-world behaviour (parent-confirmed missions), which it already does. It should not claim to reduce ADHD symptoms.
- The two trials closest to Apex's goal (RAGE-Control and Plan-It Commander) both used the game as an **adjunct**: RAGE-Control alongside a therapist, Plan-It alongside treatment as usual. Neither tested the game alone. RAGE-Control's benefit appeared in observed behaviour (aggression, oppositionality), not in felt anger. This is a reasonable target for Apex too: "doing the tool when angry", not "never feeling angry".
- Apex differs from RAGE-Control in one important way. RAGE-Control's active ingredient (versus sham) was real heart-rate feedback, because the sham arm had the same game and therapy. A sensorless Apex is therefore closer to the RAGE-Control **sham** condition, which still improved alongside ACT. Any benefit from Apex is more likely to come from rehearsal, metaphor and parent involvement than from biofeedback.
- EndeavorRx's frustration and emotional-reaction adverse events (3%/1%) show that even carefully designed games can upset some children with ADHD. Monitoring is worthwhile.

### Gaps
- I did not find a published replication of RAGE-Control beyond the 2021 RCT, or any UK trial of it.
- I could not retrieve the full text of Plan-It Commander's effect sizes or teacher-rated outcomes (PMC blocked).
- I did not retrieve the NICE NG87 (2018, updated 2019) position on digital or cognitive-training interventions in this session. Other researchers may cover it.

## 2. Biofeedback without sensors: can a game that cannot measure breathing still help? Evidence on paced-breathing apps for children

### Takeaway
Slow paced breathing (about 6 breaths/min) is the active ingredient in HRV biofeedback, and school-age children can learn it. However, the controlled paediatric evidence mostly involves sensor-based biofeedback. I found no RCT of a sensorless paced-breathing game for children with ADHD. The RAGE-Control sham arm (same game, no physiological link, plus therapy) suggests the non-biofeedback parts carry some benefit, but real feedback added measurable value.

### Cited Findings
- HRV biofeedback works through slow paced breathing at about 6 breaths/min (~0.1 Hz, "resonance frequency"). Slow-paced breathing is "an accessible way" to increase HRV and support emotion regulation. — [Blum et al., PMC6763967](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6763967/)
- [Controlled school study] Breath-focused HRV biofeedback programme in Spanish primary schools (about 6 breath cycles/min) improved emotional self-regulation. — [PMC9099602](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9099602/); and reduced anxiety/social stress — [PMC9407856](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9407856/) (abstract-level; design details not verified)
- [RCT, small] VR vs 2D heart-rate biofeedback game for 9–18-year-olds with anxiety and/or mild–moderate depression (n=19 vs 20, 10 sessions). — [Virtual Reality (Springer) 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10055-025-01123-2)
- [Within-subject experiment] Gameful breathing-training app (*JMIR Serious Games* 2021, e22802). This compared physiological responses to gameful vs non-gameful breathing guidance in adults. — [JMIR Serious Games](https://doi.org/10.2196/22802) (full text could not be retrieved; adults, not children)
- RAGE-Control sham arm: wore a monitor, but HR did not affect play. Self-reported anger improved equally in both arms, and the HR-linked arm did better on behaviour. — [PubMed 34539455](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34539455/)

### Inferences
- Apex's in-for-3 / out-for-5 hold-and-release paces breathing at roughly 7.5 breaths/min, close to the resonance range. If the child actually breathes along, a physiological benefit is plausible. The game cannot confirm compliance, so parent co-play or occasional parent observation is the realistic substitute for a sensor.
- The longer exhale (5 > 3) matches common clinical paced-breathing advice. I found no specific source on exhale ratios for children in this session.
- An optional low-cost sensor, such as phone-camera PPG or a microphone breath detector, would move Apex closer to the RAGE-Control active arm. This is speculative and adds complexity.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT of sensorless paced-breathing apps for children, with or without ADHD.
- I found no evidence on whether a child "pretending" to breathe still learns the skill.

## 3. ADHD and gaming risk: problematic gaming, hyperfocus, difficulty stopping, meltdowns at transitions; UK experts on screen time

### Takeaway
ADHD is moderately associated with gaming-disorder symptoms. The data are mostly cross-sectional, so the direction of cause is unclear. UK researchers (Przybylski at Oxford, Orben at Cambridge) find that **time** on screens or games has tiny or negligible links to wellbeing for the general population. Their message is that context and type of play matter more than minutes. Practical advice on ending sessions (staged warnings, visual timers, predictable post-play routines) is consistent across sources, but it rests on practitioner opinion rather than trials.

### Cited Findings
- [MA] Koncz P, Demetrovics Z, Takacs ZK, Griffiths MD, Nagy T, Király O (2023), *Clinical Psychology Review* 106. Association between gaming-disorder (GD) and ADHD symptoms: r=0.296 (inattention r=0.306; hyperactivity r=0.266), across 41 samples. GD severity was higher in people with ADHD than without (g=0.693), and ADHD severity higher in people with GD than without (g=0.854). The authors stress the evidence is mainly cross-sectional and call for longitudinal designs and clinical diagnoses. The record is held at the University of Edinburgh; Griffiths is at Nottingham Trent (UK). — [Edinburgh Research Explorer](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/the-emerging-evidence-on-the-association-between-symptoms-of-adhd/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0272735823001010)
- [Narrative review] ADHD–gaming disorder comorbidity in children and adolescents. It cites longitudinal evidence that impulsivity and ADHD symptoms predict later pathological gaming, and that about 25% of children with ADHD met criteria for pathological gaming in one sample. — [PMC9600100](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9600100/)
- [Obs, longitudinal] Problematic online gaming partially mediated the link between ADHD traits and later depressive symptoms (29.2%), anxiety (12.3%), psychotic experiences (20.6%) and lower wellbeing (22.1%) in adolescents. — [PMC12322020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12322020/)
- [Obs, UK/Oxford] Vuorre M, Johannes N, Magnusson K, Przybylski AK (2022), *Royal Society Open Science*. Using industry telemetry from 38,935 players, the effect of time spent gaming on wellbeing was "probably too small to be subjectively noticeable and not credibly different from zero". These were adult players. — [Tilburg repository](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/time-spent-playing-video-games-is-unlikely-to-impact-well-being/); [OII news](https://www.oii.ox.ac.uk/major-new-study-finds-little-evidence-for-causal-connection-between-well-being-and-video-game-playing/)
- [Obs, UK] Orben & Przybylski: across more than 300,000 adolescents (UK/US), technology use explained at most 0.4% of variation in wellbeing. — [OII](https://www.oii.ox.ac.uk/technology-use-explains-at-most-0-4-of-adolescent-wellbeing/); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/link-between-teens-time-digital-devices-and-lower-wellbeing-too-small-merit)
- [Obs] Orben & Przybylski (2019), time-use diaries: little evidence of substantial negative associations, even for screen use before bed. — [University of Oxford news, Apr 2019](https://www.ox.ac.uk/news/2019-04-05-study-finds-screen-time-%E2%80%93-even-bed-%E2%80%93-has-little-impact-teen-wellbeing)
- [Obs] Oxford/Cardiff/Cambridge study of more than 35,000 US children: 1–2 hours/day of screen activities was associated with better psychosocial functioning than none. — [OII](https://www.oii.ox.ac.uk/moderate-use-of-screen-time-can-be-good-for-your-health-new-study-finds/)
- Orben and colleagues have also given written evidence to UK Parliament on screen use and young people. — [UK Parliament written evidence](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/18885/pdf/)
- 2026 preprint/paper (Hakman, Vuorre et al.), "Not All Play is Equal": **in-game behaviour** predicts wellbeing differences better than time. — [PDF](https://vuorre.com/bibliography/files/HTDFPRRY/hakman-etal_2026_not-all-play-is-equal-in-game-player-be.pdf) (title-level only; content not verified)
- [Op] Transitions: children with ADHD find stopping highly stimulating activities hard because of hyperfocus. Recommended practices are staged warnings (for example 10/5/2/1 min), visible countdown timers, a parent sitting with the child to finish, a predictable post-play routine, validating the feeling ("I know it's hard to stop"), and avoiding open-ended sessions. — [ADDitude: managing transitions](https://www.additudemag.com/managing-transitions-adhd-children/); [ADDitude: transition activities](https://www.additudemag.com/help-adhd-child-transition-activities/); [KidsBeWell](https://kidsbewell.com/adhd-in-children/adhd-video-game-transitions/). These are US practitioner and magazine sources, not trials.

### Inferences
- Apex's 15-minute cap, 60-minute rest, and absence of streaks or notifications go against the known drivers of problematic play. The main residual risk is the **moment of stopping**: a hard cut-off mid-"Hunt" or mid-meter could trigger exactly the meltdown the game is meant to help with. Good practice suggests:
  - an in-game countdown (for example at 3 and 1 minutes);
  - finishing at a natural endpoint (the end of a hunt or scene) rather than a mid-action cut;
  - a calm "wind-down" closing screen, ideally one final breathing round;
  - suggesting a next off-screen activity.
- "Hunts" for replay are the feature most likely to invite "one more go". Keeping them short, inside the cap and without escalating rewards fits the evidence-informed principle.
- The UK expert consensus (Przybylski, Orben) means that 15 minutes of gaming is very unlikely to harm wellbeing in itself. The ADHD-specific concern is about compulsive patterns and difficulty stopping, not minutes.

### Gaps
- I found no UK-specific trial on transition strategies off screens for children with ADHD. The advice comes from practitioners.
- I did not find statements by Przybylski or Orben specifically about children with ADHD.
- I did not search for ACAMH-specific content on gaming in ADHD in this session.

## 4. Design ethics: dark patterns, variable rewards, loss aversion; ICO Children's Code nudge standard; UK loot-box policy; implications for trophies, ranks and "pack trust going down"

### Takeaway
UK regulation focuses on nudges that extend use or harvest data, and on paid randomised rewards. It explicitly **encourages** nudges toward wellbeing, such as breaks, pause and save. Apex already avoids streaks, loot boxes, ads and notifications. The remaining ethical question is whether loss-framed mechanics like "pack trust going down" act as loss-aversion pressure on an emotionally dysregulated child. That is a design judgement; no regulation bans it.

### Cited Findings
- [Reg] ICO Age Appropriate Design Code (Children's Code), Standard 13 "Nudge techniques": "Do not use nudge techniques to lead or encourage children to provide unnecessary personal data or turn off privacy protections." The guidance cites reward loops and positive reinforcement such as likes and streaks that "extend their use" as examples of concern. — [ICO Standard 13](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/13-nudge-techniques/); [ICO code standards](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/code-standards/); [E&T on streaks/likes](https://eandt.theiet.org/2019/04/15/data-watchdog-floats-ban-social-media-nudges-kids)
- [Reg] The ICO permits and encourages wellbeing nudges: "nudging them towards supportive resources or providing tools such as pause and save buttons". For ages **6–9**, it recommends nudging toward wellbeing behaviours "like taking breaks" and providing pause and save tools. For ages **10–12**, it recommends encouraging conscious decision-making plus wellbeing nudges. — [ICO Standard 13](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/13-nudge-techniques/)
- [Reg] The Children's Code applies to "information society services" likely to be accessed by children. A home-made game used by one family is almost certainly outside its scope, but it is a useful benchmark. — [ICO code standards](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/code-standards/) (scope inference mine)
- [Reg] UK Government (DCMS) response to the loot-box call for evidence, 18 July 2022: legislation was judged "premature". Instead, DCMS convened a technical working group for industry-led protections. Its objectives: no loot-box purchases by children without parental approval, spending controls and transparency for all players, and better evidence. Progress was to be reviewed. — [GOV.UK response](https://www.gov.uk/government/calls-for-evidence/loot-boxes-in-video-games-call-for-evidence/outcome/government-response-to-the-call-for-evidence-on-loot-boxes-in-video-games); [Hansard, 18 Jul 2022](https://hansard.parliament.uk/commons/2022-07-18/debates/22071813000007/LootBoxesInVideoGames); [Written statement HLWS210](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-statements/detail/2022-07-18/hlws210)

### Inferences
- Apex's trophy system (earned only from parent-confirmed real-world missions) is a fixed, effort-contingent, non-random reward. It is the opposite of a variable-ratio loot box and fits behavioural parent-training principles (token economies).
- Ranks and progression are low-risk if they never decay. Mechanics that **take away** standing for a bad session, such as "pack trust going down", use loss aversion. For a child with emotional dysregulation, that could cause shame or anger. Safer framings:
  - trust that only rises, or that pauses instead of falling;
  - "the pack is worried" narrative cues, with no numeric loss;
  - recovery framed as easy and immediate.
  This is design inference; I found no study testing loss framing in ADHD games.
- Neutral field-note stats ("caught it early: 2 of 3") align with non-judgemental feedback. The ICO's endorsement of break nudges supports Apex's enforced rest.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve any Ofcom statement specific to loot boxes, or the outcome of the DCMS working group (industry principles published 2023). I also did not retrieve the 2020 House of Lords Gambling Committee or 2019 DCMS Select Committee recommendations on loot boxes.
- I found no empirical study on loss-framed in-game mechanics in children with ADHD.

## 5. Arousal and intensity: heartbeat sounds, red edges and screen shake; sensory sensitivity (ADHD + autism); accessibility guidance

### Takeaway
Accessibility guidance recommends making intense effects (screen shake, flashing, loud or escalating audio) **optional and independently adjustable**, with flashing kept below seizure thresholds. Apex's reduced-motion and low-intensity settings match this. One tension needs watching: rising heartbeat, growl and red edges are designed to mirror and heighten arousal, which could amplify distress in some children rather than cue them to regulate.

### Cited Findings
- [Guidance] Game Accessibility Guidelines flashing threshold: avoid more than 3 flashes per second covering 25% or more of the screen. Warn players and offer reduced-flash modes. Offer options to reduce or disable screen shake, motion blur and rapid camera motion, because these can disorient players with cognitive or vestibular conditions. — [Summary of GAG and related guidance (secondary)](https://abratabia.com/game-accessibility/); primary site [gameaccessibilityguidelines.com](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com) (not directly fetched in this session)
- [Op] Recommended pattern: separate controls per channel (flashing, music intensity, effects volume, visual filters) rather than one global accessibility toggle. — [abratabia (secondary)](https://abratabia.com/game-accessibility/)
- [Op] ADHD-gamer and sensory-friendly settings guidance emphasises cutting screen shake, flashing and audio clutter to reduce overload. — [Switchblade Gaming ADHD checklist](https://www.switchbladegaming.com/game-settings/adhd-accessibility/); [Sensory-friendly guide](https://www.switchbladegaming.com/game-settings/sensory-friendly/) (consumer blog; low weight)
- [RCT context] EndeavorRx's mild adverse events included frustration (3%) and emotional reaction (1%), showing that games can provoke negative arousal in children with ADHD. — [sponsor page](https://www.endeavorotc.com/evidence/stars-adhd-pivotal-study/)
- RAGE-Control intentionally raised challenge while requiring calm, and HR above threshold impaired play. The arousal came from task difficulty, not alarming sensory effects. Its documented effects were positive in a therapy context. — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/930620)

### Inferences
- Heartbeat, growl and red edges that rise with the meter are a "mirror" of the child's state. For some children that is a helpful externalisation ("the wolf is getting loud"). For children with auditory or visual sensitivity, which commonly co-occurs with autism and is reported in ADHD, it may escalate distress.
- Suggested mitigations, consistent with the guidance:
  - separate sliders for audio intensity, visual vignette and shake;
  - a default of moderate rather than maximum;
  - effects that **fall noticeably** as the child uses a tool, so the reward is calm, not just the absence of alarm;
  - no flashing red at seizure-risk frequencies;
  - parent observation in early sessions to see whether effects calm or escalate this particular child.

### Gaps
- I found no study testing alarm-style sensory cues (heartbeat sounds, red vignettes) on arousal in children with ADHD.
- I did not retrieve AbleGamers or the primary GAG pages directly. The GAG summary above is from secondary sources.
- I found no source quantifying sensory processing differences in ADHD in this session.

## 6. Story/metaphor framing and parent involvement in digital interventions

### Takeaway
The effective ADHD games found here wrap skills in a narrative world: Plan-It Commander's space-commander setting, RAGE-Control's arcade game played after CBT. They also link play to real-life practice with adult support. I found no trial isolating the effect of story or metaphor on generalisation. Parent or therapist involvement is a common factor in the more positive studies.

### Cited Findings
- Plan-It Commander was built as a narrative game world with missions targeting time management, planning and prosocial skills. It was co-designed with stakeholders and used alongside treatment as usual. Its outcomes were improvements in daily-life skills rated by parents. — [Bul et al. 2016 RCT](https://www.jmir.org/2016/2/e26/metrics); [development paper](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Development-and-User-Satisfaction-of-%E2%80%9CPlan-It-a-for-Bul-Franken/01c2bb34429003528081beab826f26fc5fd1bf23); [screenshot of game world](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Screenshot-of-Plan-It-Commander-game-world_fig2_295098364)
- RAGE-Control was delivered by therapists at the end of CBT sessions, so game skills were rehearsed in a context where they were explicitly taught. — [PubMed 34539455](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34539455/)
- Schultz et al. (2025) found no game element with a clear advantage, and far transfer was unconvincing. This implies that "game features" alone (including narrative) do not guarantee generalisation. — [PubMed 41122750](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41122750/)
- Doulou et al. (2025) highlight emotional and metacognitive competence gains from serious games (narrative review). — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2414-4088/9/1/8)

### Inferences
- Apex's design (a character metaphor, the child's own warning signs, trophies earned only through parent-confirmed real-world missions) mirrors the "adjunct plus real-life practice plus adult" structure of the positive trials. That structure is more defensible than the game alone.
- Externalising metaphors (naming the "rage/urge" as a creature) are a recognised technique in narrative therapy and children's CBT. I did not retrieve a source in this session, so the report writer should treat this as background, not evidence.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT isolating narrative or metaphor framing as the active ingredient for skill generalisation in ADHD.
- I found no direct meta-analytic estimate of the effect of parent involvement in digital ADHD interventions. Parent training itself is well evidenced, but that was outside this search.
