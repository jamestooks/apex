# Official UK guidance relevant to "Apex" (home-made ADHD coping game for a 9-year-old)

Research date: 12 November 2026. Access note: nice.org.uk returned HTTP 403 to every fetch, so NG87 / QS39 text could not be read first-hand. NICE content below comes from search-result extracts of NICE pages and secondary sources. Exact recommendation numbers could not be checked, so they are left out rather than guessed (see Gaps).

## 1. NICE NG87: non-pharmacological support for school-age children, digital/self-help, schools and information sharing

### Takeaway
NG87 (2018, last substantively updated 2019, with small 2024–2026 surveillance actions) puts information and support for families first for children aged 5+. That means ADHD-focused support, which can be just 1–2 sessions: education about ADHD, parenting strategies, and liaison with school (with consent). Then come environmental modifications (shorter focus periods with movement breaks, fewer distractions), parent training (mainly where there are oppositional/conduct symptoms), medication if needed, and CBT for young people whose symptoms persist. NG87 makes no recommendation for neurofeedback or cognitive training, and has nothing on apps, games or digital self-help for managing ADHD.

### Cited Findings
- NG87 recommends ADHD-focused support for parents/carers of children 5+. It "should be ADHD focused, can be group based and as few as 1 or 2 sessions", and covers "education and information on the causes and impact of ADHD, advice on parenting strategies, with consent, liaison with school, college or university, and both parents and carers if feasible." — [NICE NG87 Recommendations (search extract)](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations); [NICE NG87 PDF](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/resources/attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-diagnosis-and-management-pdf-1837699732933)
- At diagnosis, when symptoms change and at school transitions, professionals should "obtain consent and then contact the school, college or university to explain the validity of a diagnosis of ADHD and how symptoms are likely to affect school… life". — [NICE NG87 Recommendations (search extract)](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations)
- Definition of environmental modifications: "changes that are made to the physical environment in order to minimise the impact of a person's ADHD on their day-to-day life… specific to the circumstances of each person… determined from an assessment of their needs. Examples may include changes to seating arrangements, changes to lighting and noise, reducing distractions (for example, using headphones), optimising work or education to have shorter periods of focus with movement breaks." (NG87 also says these should be in place and reviewed before medication is started.) — [NICE NG87 (via search extract)](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations); [HSE Ireland model of care quoting NICE](https://adult.adhdirl.ie/hse-model-care-adhd/i/71651707/8-treatment)
- NICE's evidence summary for the sequence of care: under 5s get an ADHD-focused group parent-training programme. For 5+ the order is "group-based ADHD-focused support and environmental modifications, group or individual parent-training programmes, followed by medication if symptoms persist, followed by cognitive behavioural therapy if symptoms persist." — [NICE NG87 draft short version](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/documents/short-version-of-draft-guideline)
- CBT is suggested ("consider") for young people whose symptoms still cause significant impairment despite other treatment, including medication. Content can cover "social skills with peers, problem-solving, self-control, active listening skills, and dealing with and expressing feelings." — [NICE NG87 PDF (search extract)](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/resources/attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-diagnosis-and-management-pdf-1837699732933)
- Diet and exercise: professionals should "stress the value of a balanced diet, good nutrition and regular exercise". Do not advise eliminating artificial colourings and additives as a general treatment. Do not advise or offer fatty-acid supplements. If foods clearly link to behaviour, refer to a dietitian; a food diary can help. — [NICE NG87 Recommendations (search extract)](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations); [NICE NG87 dietary interventions evidence](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/evidence/dietary-interventions-pdf-4844210798)
- Neurofeedback and cognitive training (e.g. working-memory training) were looked at, but there was not enough evidence to recommend them. The committee made no recommendation on cognitive training. — [PMC review of non-pharmacological management (2023)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10091126); a later meta-analysis of 38 RCTs found neurofeedback did not significantly reduce core symptoms — [University of Southampton, Dec 2024](https://www.southampton.ac.uk/psychology/news/2024/12/neurofeedback-may-not-be-effective-in-reducing-adhd-symptoms.page)
- Surveillance: the September 2024 exceptional surveillance proposed linking section 1.3 (diagnosis) to the NICE digital-assessment guidance. In May 2025 NICE added that link (a HealthTech guidance link). — [NICE Sept 2024 surveillance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/evidence/september-2024-exceptional-surveillance-of-attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-diagnosis-and-management-nice-guideline-ng87-15313350637?tab=evidence)
- In July 2026, exceptional surveillance looked at adapted trauma-focused treatment for autistic people and people with ADHD (NG116, CG142, CG170, NG87). Decision: no update to any of the guidelines. — [NICE July 2026 surveillance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/evidence/july-2026-exceptional-surveillance-of-post-traumatic-stress-disorder-nice-guideline-ng116-autism-15787134541?tab=evidence)
- The NHS England independent ADHD Taskforce (Part 2, Nov 2025) recommended that NICE "scope an early value assessment (EVA) of digital products delivering improved outcomes and efficiencies for ADHD management and treatment across the pathway and settings of care". It also called for school interventions through Mental Health Support Teams and PINS (Partnerships for Inclusion of Neurodiversity in Schools), and for support "based on their needs without waiting for a diagnosis". — [NHS England Taskforce Part 2](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/report-of-the-independent-adhd-taskforce-part-2/); [Taskforce summary](https://www.healthcare-management.uk/taskforce-report-calls-systemic-changes-adhd-services)

### Inferences
- Apex's design fits closely with the low-intensity, family-level parts of NG87. It explains ADHD-type patterns in plain words, gives parents strategies (the shared phrases), offers a plan card for school liaison, and uses short focus blocks and movement breaks ("Burst & Rest", the 15-minute cap). The skills it rehearses (self-control, dealing with and expressing feelings, problem-solving) match the CBT content NICE lists. But NICE frames CBT as a clinician-delivered therapy for *young people* (older age group) after other steps. Apex should not call its content CBT.
- NICE's support for families is delivered *by professionals*. Apex has nothing in NICE to point to as an endorsed self-help or digital treatment. NG87 is silent on parent-made or digital self-help tools for managing ADHD. That is not a recommendation against them, but it means "NICE recommended" cannot be claimed. The "not a treatment" framing is correct.
- Neurofeedback and cognitive training are the closest "game-like" interventions NICE considered, and it did not recommend them. Apex should not suggest that playing improves attention or ADHD symptoms (i.e. no "brain training" claims). Its rationale should rest on rehearsing coping routines and family communication.
- Food-related content (e.g. "fuel" missions) should stick to balanced diet and regular meals. It should not suggest elimination diets or supplements.

### Gaps
- Exact NG87 recommendation numbers (e.g. for the information/support, environmental modification, parent training and CBT recommendations) could not be verified because nice.org.uk blocked access. They should be checked first-hand before quoting.
- Could not confirm whether NICE has formally started the digital-products EVA the Taskforce asked for (no NICE source found by Nov 2026).
- Could not read NG87's research recommendations or full rationale text on digital interventions.

## 2. NICE QS39 and NICE digital-technology assessments (apps/games?)

### Takeaway
NICE's only digital ADHD technology guidance covers *assessment*, not treatment. DG60 (Oct 2024, now listed as HTG729) supports QbTest alongside clinical assessment for ages 6–17. No NICE guidance assesses apps or games for managing ADHD.

### Cited Findings
- NICE diagnostics guidance DG60 "Digital technologies for assessing ADHD" (21 Oct 2024) supports QbTest alongside standard clinical assessment for children and young people aged 6–17. The committee said it "should only be used to supplement professional judgement, and not replace it." — [NICE news](https://www.nice.org.uk/news/articles/nice-recommends-digital-technology-to-help-diagnose-adhd-in-children-and-young-people); [NICE HTG729 PDF](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/htg729/resources/digital-technologies-for-assessing-attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-adhd-pdf-1809600650784709)
- AQUA trial evidence: with QbTest, more diagnostic decisions were made within 6 months. — [Qbtech blog (manufacturer, treat with caution)](https://www.qbtech.com/blog/qbtest-the-first-digital-technology-recommended-by-nice-to-assess-adhd/)
- The Taskforce's call for an EVA of digital *management/treatment* products (above) suggests no such NICE assessment existed as of Nov 2025. — [NHS England Taskforce Part 2](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/report-of-the-independent-adhd-taskforce-part-2/)

### Inferences
- There is no NICE benchmark for an ADHD self-management app or game. Apex sits outside NICE's HealthTech scope and is not a medical device as long as it makes no diagnostic or treatment claims. Using the personal "warning signs" and meter for self-awareness only, not for screening or diagnosis, keeps it clearly separate from QbTest-type tools.

### Gaps
- QS39 (ADHD quality standard, 2013) statements could not be read (403). From memory they cover diagnosis by specialists, parent training, and medication review, but this was not verified, so no QS39 statements are quoted here.
- No NICE early value assessment on ADHD apps or games was found.

## 3. NHS practical advice for parents

### Takeaway
nhs.uk (reviewed 19 March 2025, next review March 2028) gives everyday parenting advice: exercise, regular sleep, balanced diet with regular meals, calm one-at-a-time instructions, 15–20-minute task blocks with breaks, praise and reward charts, visible to-do lists, and working with the SENCo. Much of this matches Apex directly.

### Cited Findings
- "Make time for physical activities they enjoy, as exercise is a good focus for their energy"; encourage "regular sleep"; a "healthy, balanced diet" with regular mealtimes; a food diary that can be shared with school. — [NHS: Living with ADHD](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-adhd/living-with/)
- Give instructions "one at a time in a calm voice"; split tasks "like doing homework or sitting down to eat, into 15 to 20 minute slots with a break in between each slot"; use "praise when a child or young person does well, or making a reward chart"; write "a to-do list and putting it somewhere easy to see"; talk to the SENCo about adjustments. — [NHS: Living with ADHD](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-adhd/living-with/); [NHS: ADHD treatment](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-adhd/treatment/)
- "Talking therapies such as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) might be recommended to help a child or young person with ADHD with problem solving". — [NHS: ADHD treatment](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-adhd/treatment/)
- Support organisations: ADHD UK, Young Minds, ADHD Adult UK. — [NHS: Living with ADHD](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-adhd/living-with/)
- NHS trust example (Humber Children's ADHD Intervention Team) on diet. — [Humber NHS ADHD & Diet](https://connect.humber.nhs.uk/service/childrens-adhd-intervention-team/adhd-diet/)

### Inferences
- The 15-minute session cap matches the NHS "15 to 20 minute slots with a break". The shared calm phrases match "calm voice", one instruction at a time. The fridge plan card matches "somewhere easy to see". Outside-world missions confirmed by a parent are like praise or reward charts. Physical "Burst & Rest" missions match the exercise advice.
- Apex's neutral field notes (no shaming) fit the NHS emphasis on praise. Any reward in missions should be praise or acknowledgement, not something that pushes more screen time.

### Gaps
- Did not review individual CAMHS trust self-help packs in detail (only one trust diet page found). There is no NHS-wide list of approved ADHD self-help apps for children. The NHS Apps Library was closed in 2021 (from memory, not verified here).

## 4. UK screen-time guidance (RCPCH 2019, CMOs 2019, 2026 government guidance)

### Takeaway
No UK body sets a time limit for school-age children. The RCPCH (2019) and the UK CMOs (Feb 2019) focus on family-led limits, protecting sleep (no screens in the hour before bed; devices out of bedrooms), breaks from sitting, and screen-free mealtimes. Government guidance for under-5s (27 March 2026) sets limits for under-5s only and exempts SEND assistive tech. DfE guidance for ages 5–16 was announced for autumn 2026, but I could not confirm it had been published by 12 Nov 2026.

### Cited Findings
- RCPCH (Jan 2019) four questions for families: is screen time in your household controlled? Does screen use interfere with what your family want to do? Does it interfere with sleep? Can you control snacking during screen time? Screens should be avoided for an hour before bedtime. — [Childnet summary of RCPCH](https://www.childnet.com/help-and-advice/screen-time-boundaries-parents/what-the-experts-say/); [RCPCH parent fact sheet (PDF)](https://www.childnet.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/rcpch_screen_time_parent_fact_sheet_-_final.pdf)
- UK CMOs' commentary, 7 Feb 2019: the evidence was not enough to support guidance on optimal amounts of screen time. A causal link to harm was not shown, "this does not mean that there is no effect". Advice included leaving phones outside the bedroom at bedtime, screen-free mealtimes, and family conversations. — [GOV.UK CMO commentary](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-cmo-commentary-on-screen-time-and-social-media-map-of-reviews); [AOP report](https://www.aop.org.uk/ot/professional-support/health-services/2019/02/11/uk-chief-medical-officer-issues-screen-time-guidance)
- CMOs urged technology companies to take "a precautionary approach in developing structures and remove addictive capabilities" (as quoted by the ICO). — [ICO Children's Code std 5](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/5-detrimental-use-of-data/)
- Under-5s guidance (DfE/DHSC, 27 March 2026): avoid screens for under-2s except shared activities like video calls; about 1 hour a day for ages 2–5; avoid at mealtimes and before bed; watching together is better. These limits "shouldn't apply in the same way to screen-based assistive technologies used to support children with special educational needs and disabilities." — [DfE Education Hub blog, Mar 2026](https://educationhub.blog.gov.uk/2026/03/new-advice-for-parents-on-screen-time-for-young-children); [Best Start in Life](https://campaignresources.dhsc.gov.uk/campaigns/best-start-in-life/best-start-in-life-screen-time)
- On 8 June 2026 the government announced guidance on screen use for ages 5–16, due autumn 2026. It will "avoid blanket rules", help families make informed choices, and cover topics "from scrolling and social media to sleep and learning". — [GOV.UK news, 8 June 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/newguidance-on-screen-use-for-children-aged-5-16); RCPCH responded to the call for evidence — [RCPCH response (PDF, Jul 2026)](https://www.rcpch.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2026-07/rcpch_response_to_department_for_education_dfe_call_for_evidence_screen_use_by_children_aged_5_to_16.pdf)

### Inferences
- Apex's 15-minute cap, 60-minute rest, and lack of streaks, notifications and ads go further than any UK guidance requires for a 9-year-old. They fit the RCPCH/CMO stress on family control and on screens not displacing activity or sleep. The real-world missions send the child back to play and movement.
- Bedtime: the clearest UK screen advice is no screens in the hour before bed. Apex should not prompt evening Long Exhale practice *on screen*. It could suggest the off-screen version, or a bedtime cutoff the parent can set.
- The 7-year-old sibling is above the under-5s guidance age, so the 2026 limits do not apply to her.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm whether the DfE 5–16 screen-use guidance was published by Nov 2026, or what it says. This should be checked on gov.uk before finalising.
- Did not find Ofcom or Online Safety Act codes that apply to an offline, single-family game. The Online Safety Act covers user-to-user and search services. Apex has no user-to-user features, so it is very likely out of scope (inference, not verified against Ofcom text).

## 5. UK school context: SEND Code of Practice, SENCo, reasonable adjustments, EHC plans

### Takeaway
In England, the SEND Code of Practice 0–25 (2015) sets a "graduated approach" (assess, plan, do, review) for SEN Support. Parents and the child are involved, and the SENCo coordinates. A parent-made coping plan is the kind of information schools should take into account at the assess/plan stages. A diagnosis is not needed for support. Under NICE, school liaison needs consent.

### Cited Findings
- The graduated approach is a four-part cycle: Assess, Plan, Do, Review. Plan means agreeing adjustments, interventions and support, the expected impact, and a review date. Staff should talk to parents first when assessing needs. Parents and children should take part in reviews and in planning next steps. The SENCo coordinates provision and advises teachers. — [Cheshire East Local Offer](https://www.cheshireeast.gov.uk/livewell/local-offer-for-children-with-sen-and-disabilities/education/supporting-send-in-education/sen-and-disability-in-the-early-years/sen-support-the-graduated-approach.aspx); [NASEN quick guide to SEND CoP](https://www.scanningpens.co.uk/site/downloadable-resources/primary/NASEN-SEND-Code-of-practice%20summary.pdf); [SENDIASS BCP](https://sendiass4bcp.org/support/parent-carer-resources/parent-carer-resources-special-educational-needs-and-disabilities-send-support-in-schools/)
- NHS: parents should talk to the SENCo or school nurse about adjustments and homework support. — [NHS: ADHD treatment](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-adhd/treatment/)
- NICE: with consent, liaison with school is part of ADHD-focused family support, and professionals contact school at diagnosis and transitions. — [NICE NG87 Recommendations (search extract)](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations)
- The Taskforce wants support in schools "without waiting for a diagnosis" and through PINS and MHSTs. — [NHS England Taskforce Part 2](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/report-of-the-independent-adhd-taskforce-part-2/)

### Inferences
- The printable plan card (warning signs, coping tool, grown-up phrase) can act as parent input to the school's "assess/plan" stage, or as a one-page profile. Labelling it as *parent/child-authored, not clinical advice* and inviting the SENCo to review it fits the SEND CoP co-production principle.
- Sharing the card is the family's choice, consistent with NICE's consent-based school liaison. Apex should let the family decide what is printed (e.g. leave out a child's private warning signs).

### Gaps
- Could not read the SEND Code of Practice directly on gov.uk this session (paragraph numbers such as 6.44–6.56 on the graduated approach are not verified). The Equality Act 2010 reasonable-adjustments duty (whether ADHD counts as a disability depends on the child's circumstances) and EHC plan thresholds were not researched first-hand. Note that SEND reform (Schools White Paper / SEND reforms 2025–26) may change the framework. Its status as of Nov 2026 was not checked.
- Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have different frameworks (ASN/ALN), which were not covered.

## 6. ICO Age Appropriate Design Code (Children's Code)

### Takeaway
Strictly, the Code applies only to "information society services" that process personal data and are likely to be used by children. A free, offline game made for one family that keeps all data on the device is probably outside its legal scope. Even so, its standards are the UK benchmark, and Apex already meets most of them. The relevant ones are best interests (1), detrimental use / no sticky features (5), high-privacy defaults (7), data minimisation (8), no data sharing (9), parental controls with age-appropriate transparency (11), no profiling (12), and no nudges (13).

### Cited Findings
- 15 standards: best interests; DPIAs; age-appropriate application; transparency; detrimental use of data; policies and community standards; default settings; data minimisation; data sharing; geolocation; parental controls; profiling; nudge techniques; connected toys and devices; online tools. It is a statutory code under s.123 of the Data Protection Act 2018. — [ICO Children's Code](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/)
- Scope: ISS "normally provided for remuneration, at a distance, by electronic means". Free ad-funded apps count, and non-profit services count if they are "economic activity in a more general sense". The Code applies to "processing of personal data to which the GDPR applies". The threshold is that access by children is "more probable than not". — [ICO: Services covered](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/services-covered-by-this-code/)
- Defaults "must be 'high privacy' by default"; "Only the minimum amount of personal data should be collected and retained"; nudges "should not be used to encourage children to provide unnecessary personal data, weaken or turn off their privacy settings". — [ICO Children's Code](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/)
- Standard 5: "Do not use children's personal data in ways that have been shown to be detrimental to their wellbeing, or that go against… Government advice." Sticky features include "reward loops, continuous scrolling, notifications and auto-play". The ICO suggests "pause buttons which allow children to take a break at any time without losing their progress" and "age appropriate content to support conscious choices about taking breaks". Do not offer "personalised in-game advantages… in return for extended play". — [ICO std 5](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/5-detrimental-use-of-data/)
- Standard 11: if you provide parental controls, give the child age-appropriate information about them. Controls can impede children's "rights to association, play, access to information and freedom of expression", so children should be told about monitoring in language they understand. — [ICO Children's Code (via search extract)](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/code-standards/)

### Inferences
- As built (offline, on-device, free, not offered to the public), Apex is probably not an ISS under the Code. If it were shared publicly (e.g. hosted for other families), it might come into scope if it then processes personal data, such as stored warning signs or logs. Even then, local-only storage keeps the exposure small.
- Even voluntarily, Apex matches the Code well: no streaks, notifications or ads (std 5/13); a session cap and rest break (std 5's "conscious choices about breaks"); on-device data (std 8/9); no profiling (std 12). The gaps to close are: (a) a clear, child-friendly explanation that a grown-up can see the Grown-ups log and plan cards (std 11 transparency); (b) a pause that keeps progress (std 5); (c) making sure the "instinct takes over" or meter mechanics never shame the child or push extra play (best interests, std 1).
- The 7-year-old sibling needs age-appropriate wording (std 3). Separate profiles also stop siblings seeing each other's warning signs.

### Gaps
- Did not check whether the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 changed the Code or ICO children's guidance by 2026.
- Did not verify the exact wording of the Code's standard 11 (fetched content was partial). The quote comes from a search extract of ICO pages.
