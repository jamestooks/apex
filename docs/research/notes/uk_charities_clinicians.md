Scope note: This covers UK charities (YoungMinds, Anna Freud, ADHD UK, ADHD Foundation, SPACE Herts), UK clinicians/educators (Dr Max Davie, Prof Amanda Kirby, Dr Mine Conkbayir, Prof Susan Young, Dr Sami Timimi), NICE NG87, and one UK research strand (Sonuga-Barke's delay aversion). Most material is **opinion/practice guidance**, not trial evidence; where something is research it is labelled [RESEARCH]. ## 1. What UK charities/clinicians advise parents on meltdowns, co-regulation, routines, praise/rewards, language, shame, siblings and school

### Takeaway
UK guidance points the same way: stay calm and regulate yourself first, accept the feeling but set limits on the behaviour, plan coping strategies *together when calm*, repair and move on afterwards without dwelling, rely mostly on structure and positive feedback rather than sanctions, explain ADHD to siblings, and work closely with school. Apex's Long Exhale, signal-agreement, repair scene, reflection question and home/school plan cards map closely onto this advice.

### Cited Findings
**Meltdowns and anger (YoungMinds, "Anger" parent guide, reviewed April 2024)** — [YoungMinds](https://www.youngminds.org.uk/parent/parents-a-z-mental-health-guide/anger/)
- During: "Avoid responding with anger yourself. This is likely to escalate the situation." Stay relaxed, calm voice, open body language.
- Separate the feeling from the behaviour: model script "it's okay that you're angry, but it's not okay to shout at me."
- Before: work out triggers together (journalling or mood tracking); help the child *choose* coping strategies that suit them, either active (running, sport, ripping paper) or calming (drawing, breathing); "When things are calm, think together about what happens when they're angry."
- After: check in gently once calm. Parent models accountability ("I'm sorry I got angry earlier. I was feeling tired and stressed."). "Move forward": avoid dwelling and get back to normal.
- Search summaries of the same guidance also mention agreeing a **signal** the child can use when anger is building and physical outlets (punchbag, pillow, running to the end of the garden). I saw these only in search snippets, not on the fetched page, so check them before quoting.

**Co-regulation (Dr Mine Conkbayir, UK early-years neuroscience educator)**
- "Self-regulation cannot develop without co-regulation taking place first." Children develop self-regulation through repeated experiences of being co-regulated, not by being taught it. They also need simple teaching about the brain-body link and what dysregulation feels like, plus words for their feelings — [Nord Anglia summary of Conkbayir](https://www.nordangliaeducation.com/bisb-boston/news/2024/1/12/teaching-kids-emotional-regulation); [Conkbayir LinkedIn post, c. Jan 2024](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/dr-mine-conkbayir_as-i-find-myself-reiterating-too-frequently-activity-7153275489934065664-jhfj)
- "We cannot co-regulate if we cannot self-regulate." The effectiveness of co-regulation "is heavily dependent on your emotional state and mental health at the time"; "emotions drive behaviour" (9 May 2023) — [The Voice of Early Childhood](https://thevoiceofearlychildhood.com/self-regulation-co-regulation-and-mental-health/)

**Routines, praise, rewards, communication, siblings, school (Anna Freud Centre, undated page, no citations)** — [Anna Freud](https://www.annafreud.org/resources/family-wellbeing/supporting-treatments/supporting-children-and-young-people-with-adhd/)
- Routines help the child focus. Break instructions into single steps and set achievable goals.
- Focus on "positive conversations and rewards, rather than looking at the negatives and commenting on bad behaviours"; give positive rewards for completed tasks.
- "Ask questions about their experiences rather than statements or your own opinions about it." Understand ADHD from the child's perspective and normalise it.
- Siblings: explain ADHD clearly, allow questions, and teach siblings coping strategies.
- School: keep in regular contact; suggest seating, mini-lesson goals and fidget tools.
- Parents' own wellbeing: caring for a child with ADHD "can be tiring and draining".

**Dr Max Davie (UK community paediatrician, former RCPCH officer for health promotion), ARCS Lambeth, 25 Oct 2017 (older than the preferred date range)** — [ARCS Lambeth](https://www.arcslambeth.co.uk/my-child-has-adhd-is-it-my-fault/)
- Five pillars: "Positive responses to any positive behaviour"; "A few clear, fair, consistent rules"; good sleep; "Regular meals together"; "Time exercising most or every day".
- "You can't change the fact that your child has ADHD, and you didn't make it happen." Put blame, shame and guilt to one side. The environment shapes how troublesome ADHD becomes: a school that gives exercise, adapts teaching and explicitly praises effort gets a happier pupil than a strict, indifferent one.
- Treating behavioural ability as part of the child's profile and "not their fault" means the child "can be rewarded for effort on their own terms" — [ARCS Lambeth, related Davie piece](https://www.arcslambeth.co.uk/its-not-his-fault-hes-got-adhd/) (from a search snippet; page not fetched)

**After-school restraint collapse (SPACE Hertfordshire neurodiversity charity, 8 Sep 2022)** — [SPACE Herts](https://spaceherts.org.uk/2022/09/08/after-school-restraint-collapse/)
- Children mask at school and release it with their safe person. "Remember that they aren't choosing to meltdown… meet them with compassion."
- Snacks (crunchy food), "Let them take the lead – don't speak to them until they are ready", heavy work, the park, trampolining. Ask school about sensory breaks or an earlier or alternative pick-up.
- Other UK sources (in search snippets) add removing demands after school, giving food within minutes, and **keeping siblings apart** if needed (for example in the car). They also note siblings can feel frustrated, confused and angry, and point to YoungSibs (Contact/Sibs) — [NeuroTribe UK](https://neurotribe.uk/blog/the-after-school-meltdown-understanding-and-surviving-our-kids-after-school-restraint-collapse/); [Adoption UK](https://www.adoptionuk.org/Blogs/latest-blogs/after-school-restraint-collapse)

**NICE NG87 (2018, updated 2019) — the formal UK position [GUIDELINE, evidence-graded]**
- Offer an ADHD-focused group parent-training programme as first-line treatment for parents of under-5s. NICE's evidence review found "a clinically important benefit on some measures of symptoms" — [NICE NG87 recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations) (from a search summary; full page returned 403)
- I could not verify the exact wording for children aged 5 and over (information and support for parents, group parent training where ODD/CD symptoms are present, environmental modifications) because the page was blocked.

### Inferences
- Apex's design lines up well with UK advice here: a named signal ("Wait for the signal"), coping tools the child picks and rehearses when calm, a repair scene that mirrors the parent-apology model, a reflection question ("what happened just before?") that matches trigger-spotting, and plan cards that support home-school consistency.
- Conkbayir's point that a parent cannot co-regulate unless they are regulated themselves suggests the parent phrases and the parent log should prompt the *parent* to regulate first, for example "take your own exhale, then say it", rather than treating the phrase as a tool to switch off the child's behaviour.
- YoungMinds' "move forward, avoid dwelling" is a reason to keep the repair scene and failure cards short. One next step, as Apex already does, fits.
- Sibling guidance (explain ADHD, give the sibling her own coping tools) suggests the 7-year-old should be framed as a player with her own instincts, not a helper or monitor of her brother. Shared phrases should never become something one sibling can use against the other ("your rage meter's rising!").

### Gaps
- RCPsych parent leaflet (ADHD and hyperkinetic disorder) could not be fetched (404). Its current wording on praise and consequences is not verified.
- ADDISS, Mind, Contact and Place2Be pages were not reached within the tool budget.
- No UK charity source found that specifically addresses therapeutic games or apps for ADHD children.

## 2. Neurodiversity-affirming language in the UK: strengths, "superpower", "disorder", and "instincts to master/tame"

### Takeaway
UK voices are split. The ADHD Foundation (Dr Tony Lloyd) strongly backs strengths framing and "superpower" language. ADHD UK and clinicians such as Prof Susan Young and Prof Amanda Kirby warn against "superpower" talk that is not balanced by the real difficulties, and against assuming one label brings a fixed set of gifts. Critical psychiatrists (Timimi) dispute neurological framings altogether. Apex's line "your senses never switch off — a gift" lands between these camps and is safest when paired with the cost.

### Cited Findings
- ADHD Foundation CEO Dr Tony Lloyd has said the aim is to tell young people that being neurodiverse is "not just 'ok' but also a 'superpower'" (2021, comic launch) — [Good News Liverpool](https://www.goodnewsliverpool.co.uk/2021/03/18/adhd-foundation-launches-inspirational-comic/); see also his 2022 expert opinion paper on neurodiversity as a paradigm — [ADHD Foundation PDF, March 2022](https://www.adhdfoundation.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Neurodiversity-An-expert-opinion-Dr-Tony-Lloyd-14th-March-2022-SFI-Health.pdf) (not fetched in full)
- ADHD UK's language style guide (undated) says avoid "ADHD is a super-power" *without qualifying how challenging ADHD can be*. It can be very hard for someone who is struggling to be told they have a superpower, so ask people how they want it described. Avoid "suffers from ADHD"; use "has ADHD". It calls ADHD "a formally recognised disability" — [ADHD UK style guide](https://adhduk.co.uk/about-us/press/adhd-uk-adhd-language-style-guide-reccomendations-on-writing-about-adhd/)
- ADHD UK CEO Henry Shelford (who has ADHD) keeps "disorder" as a medical term meaning symptoms impair functioning across several areas of life — [ADHD UK deep dive on emotional dysregulation](https://adhduk.co.uk/deep-dives/episode-1-deep-dive-into-adhd-and-emotional-dysregulation-with-henry-shelford-and-dr-shyamal-mashru/) (from a search snippet)
- Prof Susan Young (UK clinical and forensic psychologist), 14 Aug 2023: "I don't know anyone who I treat who would agree that ADHD is a superpower, because that completely undermines the struggle". She also warns that on TikTok ADHD "is presented as a quirky thing… it's almost become entertainment", and says "Pills don't build skills" — [The Psychologist (BPS)](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/adhd-presented-quirky-thing-its-almost-become-entertainment)
- Prof Amanda Kirby (UK, former Professor of Developmental Disorders, Do-IT Solutions): "Neurodiversity is all about people not labels". She rejects attributing one set of skills to everyone with a given diagnosis, describes people as having "spiky profiles", and argues that giving a specific diagnostic label to someone's thinking is inaccurate and pathologising — [Kirby, LinkedIn, "we all are 'super' but we don't all know it or can show it"](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/neurodiversity-we-all-super-dont-know-can-show-prof-amanda-kirby); [Kirby, "spiky profiles"](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/neurodiversity-lets-embrace-our-spiky-profiles-prof-amanda-kirby) (search summaries; undated)
- Dr Sami Timimi (UK consultant child psychiatrist, critical voice) argues that diagnostic descriptions such as ADHD or "emotional dysregulation" can let the *context* causing distress off the hook. He questions the lifelong-condition and medication-first models and challenges the idea of a clear neurological split between neurodivergent and neurotypical people. His 2025 book is *Searching for Normal* — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sami_Timimi); [HGI book review](https://www.hgi.org.uk/articles/searching-for-normal/); [Mad in the UK, Feb 2026](https://www.madintheuk.com/2026/02/there-is-no-over-or-under-diagnosis-of-adhd-or-autism-just-a-confused-cacophony-of-opinions/) (title only, not fetched)
- UK debate in 2026: ADHD UK complained publicly about Channel 4's documentary "The Great ADHD Myth?", saying it "piled stigma onto the community". This shows how charged UK public discussion is — [ADHD UK, 20 Aug 2026](https://adhduk.co.uk/2026/08/20/bbc-adhd-charity-complains-about-channel-4-documentary/); [Care Appointments](https://careappointments.com/care-news/england/236551/adhd-charity-says-channel-4-documentary-piled-stigma-onto-the-community/)
- Conkbayir argues that self-regulation is "diametrically opposed to behaviour management strategies" and criticises "quick-wins of behaviour management" that work through threats or humiliation — [The Voice of Early Childhood, 2023](https://thevoiceofearlychildhood.com/self-regulation-co-regulation-and-mental-health/)

### Inferences
- **"Instincts" framing:** I found no UK source that comments directly on animal or instinct metaphors. Read against the sources above:
  - Positive reading: it externalises the difficulty ("the rage instinct", not "you are bad"). It treats intense feelings as functional ("rage tells you something matters"), which fits YoungMinds' feel-versus-do split and Davie's "not their fault". Mastery through skill fits Young's "pills don't build skills".
  - Likely pushback: "master/tame" could read as compliance-focused, implying the child's natural self is wild and must be subdued, which is what Conkbayir and affirming voices criticise. Applying it to every ADHD trait (energy, distraction) risks presenting the traits themselves as the problem, rather than a mismatch with the environment (Timimi, Kirby).
  - Mitigation consistent with the sources: say the instincts are not removed or beaten but **understood and steered**, for example "work with" rather than "tame". Show that the world, and the mentor's or pack's support, changes too, not just the raptor. Let some instincts win in the right context, such as energy as a hunting advantage.
- **"Gift" lines:** to meet ADHD UK's guidance, pair each strength with its cost in the same breath, for example "your senses never switch off — a gift on the hunt, exhausting in a crowded den". That is how Scar's "same restless brain" can stay honest. Avoid the literal word "superpower".
- Scar as a mentor who shares the condition reflects the ADHD Foundation's role-model approach (its comics). His struggle should be visible so he does not come across as a "quirky" success story (Young's warning).

### Gaps
- Leanne Maskell (ADHD coach, ADHD Babes) and Dr Tony Lloyd's specific parent-facing statements on language were not retrieved.
- No UK survey data found on how children with ADHD themselves receive "superpower" or strengths framing.

## 3. Rejection sensitivity and emotional intensity: handling failure and feedback

### Takeaway
UK popular ADHD voices advise telling the child openly that criticism will feel intense, praising effort and process, giving small specific evidence of competence, and being "the safe place, not the detective". Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria (RSD) is widely used in UK parent advice but is **not a formal diagnosis and has a thin research base**. Apex's "consequences, not punishment" failure cards (what happened plus one next step) fit this. A visible falling "trust" meter may be a risk point.

### Cited Findings
- Alex Partridge (UK, ADHD Chatter podcast host and author; lived experience, not a clinician), HuffPost UK, 23 Mar 2026:
  - "Tell them that their emotional responses to these criticisms will be intense, but there's nothing wrong with this."
  - Praise the process so children "assign value to their effort, not just their output", and teach them to tell fact from opinion.
  - "Tell them they're not broken, just different."
  — [HuffPost UK](https://www.huffingtonpost.co.uk/entry/child-rejection-sensitivity-dysphoria-parenting-advice_uk_69ba7299e4b08059575e3e34)
- UK parenting-site guidance (non-clinical, from search snippets): RSD can be triggered by *perceived* rejection; parents should be "the safe place children land, not the detective"; "Big compliments bounce off children who have heard years of corrections; small, specific evidence of capability is what sticks" — [ADHD Pathfinder](https://www.adhdpathfinder.co.uk/post/rejection-sensitive-dysphoria-in-children-and-teens-with-adhd); [Nurture ADHD](https://www.nurtureadhd.com/rejection-sensitive-dysphoria-adhd/) (UK origin of Nurture ADHD not confirmed)
- Emotional dysregulation is a recognised part of ADHD in UK charity discussion — [ADHD UK deep dive with Dr Shyamal Mashru](https://adhduk.co.uk/deep-dives/episode-1-deep-dive-into-adhd-and-emotional-dysregulation-with-henry-shelford-and-dr-shyamal-mashru/)
- Davie: explicitly praise effort, and reward effort "on their own terms" — [ARCS Lambeth](https://www.arcslambeth.co.uk/my-child-has-adhd-is-it-my-fault/)

### Inferences
- "Takeover" consequences and falling trust should be reversible quickly and visibly, and explained in neutral words ("Rage took over. The herd scattered. Next time: Long Exhale before the charge."). That matches the "say what happened plus one next step" design.
- A falling trust meter for Scar or the pack could be felt as *relational rejection*, the very trigger RSD advice warns about. Safer options:
  - Scar's trust never falls; only a task or resource state does ("the herd is spooked").
  - Show the repair route straight away.
- Process praise should name the specific tool used ("You used the exhale before the charge"), not just "Great job". Apex's in-game feedback and the parent phrases can follow this.
- Telling the child in advance that "failing a mission will feel big, and that's your brain, not you" could sit in Scar's introduction.

### Gaps
- No UK clinician or charity primary source found on RSD specifically (it is mostly a US term from Dodson). No peer-reviewed UK evidence on RSD found.
- No UK guidance found on failure states in games for ADHD children.

## 4. Rewards: charts, immediacy, intrinsic and extrinsic motivation

### Takeaway
UK guidance (Anna Freud, Davie, NICE-endorsed parent training) supports positive reinforcement and rewards for effort. UK research (Sonuga-Barke's delay aversion) supports making rewards **immediate and frequent**, because children with ADHD discount delayed rewards more steeply. Apex's trophy (not money or screen time) awarded after a parent confirms an outside mission is in line with this, provided the parent confirms promptly and the game also gives small immediate acknowledgements.

### Cited Findings
- Anna Freud: emphasise positive conversations and rewards for completed tasks over commenting on bad behaviour — [Anna Freud](https://www.annafreud.org/resources/family-wellbeing/supporting-treatments/supporting-children-and-young-people-with-adhd/)
- Davie: "Positive responses to any positive behaviour"; reward effort — [ARCS Lambeth, 2017](https://www.arcslambeth.co.uk/my-child-has-adhd-is-it-my-fault/)
- [RESEARCH] Delay aversion (Sonuga-Barke, UK, from 1992 onwards): children with ADHD choose smaller, sooner rewards over larger, later ones more than controls. Some ADHD behaviour may be motivated by escaping the negative feelings that delay causes — [Core.ac.uk review "Delay Aversion and ADHD"](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/12827912.pdf); meta-analysis confirming the effect on choice-delay and temporal discounting tasks — [Marx, Hacker, Yu, Cortese & Sonuga-Barke, J Atten Disord 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1087054718772138); neural correlates — [Van Dessel et al., JCPP 2018](https://acamh.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcpp.12868)
- UK parenting guidance (non-clinical): "Consequences only work at the point of performance"; small, immediate, related consequences work within a plan built mostly on structure, connection and reinforcement — [Nurture ADHD](https://www.nurtureadhd.com/discipline-child-with-adhd/) (from a search snippet)
- Conkbayir criticises reward-and-sanction behaviour management as opposed to developing self-regulation — [The Voice of Early Childhood, 2023](https://thevoiceofearlychildhood.com/self-regulation-co-regulation-and-mental-health/)

### Inferences
- Delay aversion implies that outside missions confirmed days later will motivate weakly. Better options:
  - Instant in-game acknowledgement when the child logs the attempt (a Scar line or mark), with parent confirmation at the same moment if possible.
  - "Waiting for the signal" missions could be deliberately short and build up gradually, since they rehearse tolerating delay.
- Keeping trophies symbolic, story-linked and separate from screen time or money avoids the token-economy battles affirming voices dislike. The parent-confirmation step, though, makes the parent a judge. To stay on the co-regulation side, make confirmation a celebration conversation, not an audit.
- Conkbayir-style voices would likely accept trophies framed as *recognition of a skill used* rather than payment for compliance.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve UK primary guidance on reward charts specifically (RCPsych leaflet, NHS pages). NICE's description of parent-training content (which typically includes praise, rewards and planned ignoring) was not verified.
- No UK evidence found on whether extrinsic rewards undermine intrinsic motivation in ADHD children specifically. The European ADHD Guidelines Group meta-analyses on behavioural interventions (Sonuga-Barke/Daley, around 2013–14) were not retrieved, so I cannot cite them here.

## 5. Recurring messages of named UK figures and practical home strategies (routines, visual supports, bedtime, transitions, restraint collapse)

### Takeaway
UK figures repeat a short list: it is not the child's (or parent's) fault; structure, sleep, food, exercise and positive feedback matter more than sanctions; co-regulation comes before self-regulation; strengths should be recognised but not oversold; and home and school should be consistent. For home strategies, routines worked out *with* the child, single-step instructions, decompression after school, and calm, low-demand handling of meltdowns are the common advice.

### Cited Findings
- **Dr Max Davie:** not the parent's fault; sleep, family meals, daily exercise, a few consistent rules, positive responses — [ARCS Lambeth](https://www.arcslambeth.co.uk/my-child-has-adhd-is-it-my-fault/)
- **Dr Mine Conkbayir:** co-regulation first; adult regulation determines child regulation; give children body-brain language; against punitive behaviour management — [Voice of Early Childhood](https://thevoiceofearlychildhood.com/self-regulation-co-regulation-and-mental-health/)
- **Prof Amanda Kirby:** people not labels; spiky profiles — [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/neurodiversity-lets-embrace-our-spiky-profiles-prof-amanda-kirby)
- **Dr Tony Lloyd / ADHD Foundation:** strengths and "superpower" reframing; role models — [Good News Liverpool](https://www.goodnewsliverpool.co.uk/2021/03/18/adhd-foundation-launches-inspirational-comic/)
- **Prof Susan Young:** skills-building, against "superpower" and "quirky" narratives — [BPS Psychologist](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/adhd-presented-quirky-thing-its-almost-become-entertainment)
- **Dr Sami Timimi:** focus on context and distress, sceptical of the diagnostic and medical framing — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sami_Timimi)
- **Routines:** work out and write down the routine *with* the child so they have input and buy-in. Pick your battles, protect sleep, regular meals and downtime. Talk quietly during meltdowns — [ADHD and You (pharma-sponsored UK site)](https://adhdandyou.co.uk/parent-or-carer/practical-tips-helping-home-parent) (from a search snippet); [Leicestershire Partnership NHS Trust top tips PDF, May 2020](https://www.leicspart.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/Top-tips-to-support-families-with-children-with-ADHD-at-home.pdf) (not fetched)
- **Instructions:** single steps and achievable goals — [Anna Freud](https://www.annafreud.org/resources/family-wellbeing/supporting-treatments/supporting-children-and-young-people-with-adhd/)
- **After-school decompression:** low demands, snack, child leads, movement — [SPACE Herts](https://spaceherts.org.uk/2022/09/08/after-school-restraint-collapse/)

### Inferences
- The printable plan cards suit the advice to "write down the routine with your child". Co-creating them (the child picks his tools and wording) would add the buy-in the advice stresses.
- Session timing matters. Running Apex as an after-school "first thing" activity may land during restraint collapse, so the decompression advice suggests snack and movement first. The reflection question is better asked at a calm time (YoungMinds: plan when calm), not straight after a hard day.
- The movement-break mechanic matches the "daily exercise" and "heavy work" advice from Davie and SPACE.

- Leanne Maskell's recurring messages were not retrieved.
- Contact (the charity for families with disabled children) sibling guidance and YoungSibs content were not fetched directly.
